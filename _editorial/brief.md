# Editorial brief for essays and presentations

This brief defines a reusable approach for turning technical ideas into essays, lectures and related public artifacts. It is a statement of goals and method, not a template that every piece must follow mechanically.

## Purpose

Create work that helps a reader or audience change how they see and approach a problem. Information is necessary, but the finished piece should produce recognition, judgment and agency—not merely recall.

## Begin with the problem

Establish the premise before introducing analogies, history or technical detail.

The opening should let the audience answer:

- What situation are we examining?
- Why does it matter now?
- What common assumption will be tested?
- What question will the piece help me answer?

An analogy should organize an argument that has already been motivated. It should not be asked to supply the missing context by itself.

## Build one argumentative spine

A collection of correct sections is not yet an essay or a lecture. Choose one claim, question or object that can accumulate meaning as the piece advances.

Prefer a structure in which:

1. a familiar situation creates a question;
2. the first explanation reaches a limit;
3. that limit makes the next idea necessary;
4. later examples transform the reader's understanding of the opening;
5. the conclusion resolves the original question rather than merely summarizing topics.

Return to a stable example when possible. Each return should reveal something new.

## Start from lived experience

Begin with an object, choice, failure or uncertainty the audience can recognize. Introduce definitions and abstractions only after the need for them has been felt.

Technical depth should support the argument. It should not become a test of whether the audience already belongs to the field.

## Prefer tensions to topics

“Improve X” is usually a theme, not a problem. Look for two legitimate demands pulling in different directions, a boundary that excludes an important interaction, or two explanations that cannot both remain unchanged.

A useful problem frame identifies:

- the tension;
- why each side exists;
- a feasible way into the problem;
- who or what changes if it is resolved;
- what observation could show the proposed account is wrong.

## Make systems visible

When a component appears to behave irrationally, widen the boundary. Show inputs, outputs, feedback, neighboring components and the objective being optimized.

Avoid using “systems thinking” as a slogan. Demonstrate it by showing an interaction that a component-level account omits.

## Use mathematics as a way of seeing

Present mathematics through three transferable habits:

- **Abstraction:** discard details that do not affect the relation of interest.
- **Generalization:** solve a family of problems rather than one instance.
- **Structure:** recognize the same relation beneath different names or domains.

Calculations and notation are useful when they reveal one of these habits. They are not automatically evidence of depth.

## Use AI without surrendering judgment

AI can expand access to explanations, literature, code, counterexamples and alternate formulations. The author remains responsible for:

- selecting the question;
- defining the boundary and criterion;
- checking claims and sources;
- distinguishing a plausible answer from a justified one;
- deciding what the audience should carry away.

Avoid claims that knowledge, expertise or infrastructure have become universally equal. State precisely which cost has fallen and which constraint remains.

## Teach through prediction and revision

Whenever practical, ask the audience to predict before revealing an answer. A prediction creates a model that can be corrected; passive explanation does not.

The learning loop is:

> hypothesize → test → observe → revise

Call this a feedback loop or iterative error correction. Do not borrow a mathematical term such as “gradient ascent” unless its technical conditions genuinely apply.

## Use stories as evidence, not ornament

Historical and personal stories should earn a specific claim. Include the detail that changes the argument and omit biography that does not.

Be careful with heroic-isolation stories, compressed histories and claims about intention. Preserve uncertainty rather than completing a satisfying story with unsupported detail.

## Design the emotional movement

A strong piece usually changes not only what the audience knows but how the problem feels. Useful movements include:

- familiarity → surprise;
- intimidation → permission;
- confidence → productive doubt;
- confusion → a usable distinction;
- passive agreement → an action the audience can perform.

Do not end at inspiration alone. Give the audience a criterion, question or practice they can use.

## Separate source from artifact

Keep editable source, contextual notes and references under version control. Publish only the artifacts intended for readers.

For each substantial piece, prefer:

- one canonical source document;
- one concise editorial-context file explaining its durable premise and guardrails;
- stable citations close to the claims they support;
- reproducible diagrams or self-contained artifacts where practical;
- a rendered output that is verified before publication.

Development archaeology—discarded plans, alternate orders, critique logs and generated build directories—does not need to accompany the canonical source unless it has continuing editorial value.

## Completion test

Before publishing, ask:

- Is the problem clear before the first analogy?
- Can the central argument be stated in one or two sentences?
- Does every major example earn a claim?
- Does each section make the next section necessary?
- Are strong claims qualified to the level supported by evidence?
- Are sources attached to factual and historical assertions?
- Does the ending resolve the opening?
- Does the audience leave with something they can do?
